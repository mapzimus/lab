The Century of Africa

The world will add roughly two billion people before 2100. Africa will add more than that on its own, because most of the rest of the planet has started to shrink. This is a map of where those people arrive, the cities they build, and what it would take to connect them.

Chapter 1 / 4

The population century

The UN counts births and deaths in every country and projects them forward. Everything here uses their middle estimate, the one they consider most likely, rather than the high or low bound. On that path, the balance of the world tips steadily in one direction.

2025: one in five

About 1.55 billion people live in Africa today, one of every five humans alive. The continent already carries more weight than most mental maps allow. Nigeria has more people than Russia. Ethiopia has more than Japan.

Countries shaded by population in 2025. The rest of the world sits on the same scale, dimmed so Africa reads first.

2100: closer to two in five

By the end of the century Africa reaches 3.8 billion, better than a third of everyone alive. Europe finishes smaller than it started. Asia peaks around 2055 and turns down. China ends the century with fewer than half the people it has today. Africa is the only region still climbing when the clock runs out.

Regional totals, 1950 to 2100. The shaded band around Africa is the UN's own low to high range, which reaches from 2.7 to 5.3 billion by 2100.

The multiplication map

Same countries, different question: how many times over does each one grow? Angola and DR Congo land near four times their current size, with Tanzania just behind. The blue countries go the other way. Italy, Spain and Japan all finish the century well below where they stand now.

Why this is not a guess

Projections like these are not predictions about how people will behave. They are mostly arithmetic on an age pyramid. The map now shows the median age of every country. In Niger it is 15.6 years, in DR Congo 15.8, in Somalia 15.6. In Japan it is 49.8 and in Italy 48.2.

Women in Niger have 5.8 children on average today, and in DR Congo 5.9, against 1.5 in Germany. Those numbers are falling almost everywhere and the UN expects them to keep falling. Growth continues anyway, because the women who will have children in 2050 have already been born. Demographers call it momentum, and it is most of what this map is showing.

The overtaking years

Rankings are how most of us carry a sense of national weight, so here is the moment the rankings flip. Each line is the year an African country passes a Western one and never falls back behind it.

Chapter 2 / 4

Where the megacities rise

People do not spread out evenly. They gather. Every circle on this map is one urban area, sized by the people in it and tracked from 1975 to 2050.

1975: six cities

Six African urban areas held a million people. Cairo was the only large one, at 7.3 million. Lagos had 2.5 million, Kinshasa barely one, Dar es Salaam fewer than 300,000. Tokyo alone held 24 million that year, more than double the three of them put together.

2025: the arrival

Eighty-eight African urban areas now clear a million people. Cairo is at 25 million, Lagos 13, Kinshasa 11, every one of them larger than Paris or London. Follow the coast east from Abidjan and the circles begin to touch: twelve cities of a million or more inside 830 km of shoreline, on their way to 56 million people between them.

2050: past the West

Cairo reaches 32 million by mid century and Luanda 20. New York sits about where it has sat for fifty years, near 13 million, and Tokyo is falling. Africa will hold 129 cities above a million people, and most of them are names that have never run in a Western headline.

2100: cities with no precedent

The UN's city projections stop at 2050. Two researchers, Daniel Hoornweg and Kevin Pope, carried the curves to the end of the century and landed on numbers with no parallel in history: Lagos near 88 million, Kinshasa 84, Dar es Salaam 74. Lagos on its own would be larger than Germany. Kinshasa would just about match it.

The hollow rings are those 2100 figures. They are drawn hollow because they are one team's projection rather than a count, and they sit at the high end of what other researchers estimate.

Chapter 3 / 4

Connecting a continent

Cities that size have to be joined to each other. This chapter lays three maps over one another: what the continent inherited, what is going into the ground right now, and what the demand of 2050 implies.

What the map inherited

African railways were mostly laid to carry copper, cocoa and oil from the interior to the nearest port, not to carry people between cities. The lines run seaward, and they rarely meet each other. Kinshasa and Lubumbashi are the two largest cities in DR Congo, about 1,570 km apart, with no railway between them.

Who is building, and why

The headline railways of the past decade were financed and built by China: Mombasa to Nairobi, Addis Ababa to the port of Djibouti, Lagos to Ibadan. Beijing has read the same population tables as everyone else and moved first. In 2023 the United States and the European Union answered with the Lobito Corridor, an old line rebuilt from the Angolan coast into the copper belt. Whoever lays the track tends to set the trade routes for a century.

Where the traffic wants to go

Transport planners estimate future flow with a gravity model: two cities pull on each other in proportion to their populations, and against the square of the distance between them. Run it across the thirty largest African cities of 2050 and the strongest links draw themselves. The West African coast. The Nile. A ladder up through the Great Lakes. Kinshasa reaching for Luanda.

A map of demand, not a construction plan. Terrain, borders and money are not in the model.

The lights are already on

Satellites have photographed the earth at night for decades. This is 2020, and the lit ground follows the same lines the model drew: the coastal strip, the Nile, the eastern highlands. These corridors are not a forecast waiting to begin. They are lit tonight.

Harmonized DMSP and VIIRS nighttime lights, 2020 (Li et al.), with the modeled corridors and the built railways still faintly drawn underneath.

What the lights leave out

A map of light is also a map of who has power, so here is that map directly: the share of people in each country with electricity at home. Across Africa 623 million people live without it, and 454 million without a basic supply of drinking water.

DR Congo is the sharpest version of the problem. It is building toward a capital of 84 million people, and today 78% of Congolese have no electricity and two in three have no safe water. Nothing in this story says those services will arrive on time. The cities are coming either way, which is exactly why the question matters.

World Bank open data, most recent year available for each country, generally 2022 or 2023.

Chapter 4 / 4

Kinshasa, up close

Continental numbers stop meaning much after a while. So the last chapter lands in a single city and watches the century arrive there, decade by decade, in satellite maps of built ground.

Two capitals, one river

The Congo River widens here into a lake called the Malebo Pool. Kinshasa sits on the south bank, capital of the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Brazzaville sits on the north, capital of the Republic of the Congo. No two capital cities on earth are closer together. There is still no bridge.

1975: the city on the plan

A million people. The sand-colored ground is everything built by then, and the colonial town is still legible inside it: a dense strip along the water, a grid running inland, then farmland.

Built ground mapped from the satellite archive by the European Commission's Global Human Settlement Layer, in squares about 92 meters across.

1990 to 2000: growth without a boom

Mobutu falls, two wars follow, the economy comes apart. The city triples anyway, to 3 million by 1990 and 5.2 million by 2000. Almost none of the new ground is planned. Neighborhoods assemble themselves house by house and push south into the hills.

2010 to 2020: the megacity

Ten million people. New building runs east along the highway toward N'Djili airport and climbs slopes nobody surveyed for streets. Then look at the thin gray lines. The road network carrying those ten million is largely the one drawn for a city of one million.

2030 and past it

The satellite record carries built ground to 2030, when the UN expects 11.6 million residents. The century-end projection is 84 million, one city holding about as many people as Germany does today, standing on the ground you have just watched fill in.

It grew up, not out

Built ground across Kinshasa and Brazzaville grew about one and a half times since 1975. The population standing on that same 1975 ground went from 2.3 million to 14 million. The city did not sprawl so much as thicken. The brightest patches below carry more than 60,000 people per square kilometer, over five times the density of New York City, and almost none of it was drawn by a planner.

Population per cell from the European Commission's GHS-POP dataset, 1975 and 2025, on the same grid as the built-ground maps. The 2025 layer is modelled rather than counted.

Where the people actually went

Measure it in rings out from the old river front and the pattern is hard to miss. Built ground grew by half. The people on it grew by fourteen million, and 83% of them are standing on ground that was already built in 1975. Half the city still lives within ten kilometres of the colonial core.

Each row is a five kilometre ring. Dark is 1975, amber is today. The city did spread, but the spreading is the small half of the story.

The flat ground ran out

There is a reason the packing happened. The old city sits on a shelf of flat land by the river, at a median slope of 2 degrees, and only about one part in forty of it is steep. That land is finished. Every decade since has had to build on worse ground: the growth added by 1990 came in at twice the slope of the old city, and roughly one part in eleven of it was properly steep.

On bare tropical hillsides with no drainage, heavy rain does not run off, it digs. Kinshasa loses houses and streets to erosion gullies most wet seasons, and the map of where that happens is largely the map of where the city had nowhere flat left to go.

Slope from the Copernicus 30 m elevation model, averaged to the built-up grid. The 2030 layer is the JRC's projection and covers a small area, so its slope figures move around more than the rest.

Streets built for a tenth of the city

Chapter by chapter this story has shown the ground filling in. Here is what did not. Counting every street inside the built-up area, the network grew about 37% between the 1975 footprint and the 2030 one. Over the same stretch the city went from roughly one million people to more than eleven.

Per person, that is a fall from about nine metres of street to one. Nothing collapsed and nothing was demolished. The city simply arrived, and the streets did not.

Streets from OpenStreetMap, measured inside each epoch's built footprint. OSM maps the network as it is now, so the early years are generous: some of those streets were not there yet, which makes the real decline steeper than the line.

One road to the sea

Zoom out and the last problem shows up. Kinshasa sits hundreds of kilometres inland on a river that ocean ships cannot climb, because the Livingstone Falls sit between the city and the estuary. Almost everything imported for it lands at Matadi and finishes the journey on the two lines drawn here: one national road, and one railway.

That is the whole connection between a city heading for tens of millions of people and the rest of the world. Chapter 3 asked which corridors a growing continent will need. This is what the question looks like from the ground, for one city, today.

Method and sources

  • Population: UN World Population Prospects 2024, medium variant. Regional shares, country series and crossover years are computed from the annual data.
  • Cities: UN World Urbanization Prospects 2025, DEGURBA city database, 1975 to 2050. The 2100 city figures come from Hoornweg and Pope, Environment & Urbanization 29(1), 2017, and are drawn hollow because they are projections.
  • Corridors: Natural Earth 1:10m rail and roads; a curated project list (AU and UNECA Trans-African Highways, plus SGR and BRI lines per public financing records); a gravity model computed from the city data above. Built railways use their real alignments from OpenStreetMap. Planned corridors are schematic, city to city.
  • Kinshasa: JRC Global Human Settlement Layer, GHS-BUILT-S and GHS-POP R2023A (CC BY 4.0), 3 arcsecond epochs, 20 percent built threshold. Densification compares GHS-POP 1975 with 2025 inside the 1975 footprint. River and roads © OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL).
  • Services: World Bank open data (CC BY 4.0), access to electricity and to basic drinking water, most recent year per country in 2015 to 2023.
  • Night lights: harmonized DMSP and VIIRS global nighttime light dataset, 2020 layer (Li, Zhou, Zhao and Zhao; figshare doi:10.6084/m9.figshare.9828827, CC BY 4.0).
  • Base: Natural Earth (public domain). Built with MapLibre GL JS. No tiles and no backend: the whole map is about 3 MB of GeoJSON.
