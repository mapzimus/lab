The Century of Africa

Almost all of the world's population growth between now and 2100 happens on one continent. Scroll to see where the people will live, which cities will grow biggest, what it takes to connect them — and what it all looks like on the ground in one city, Kinshasa.

Chapter 1 / 4

The population century

The United Nations counts every country's population and projects it forward to 2100. This chapter uses their central estimate — not the highest or lowest guess, the middle one. Watch where the weight of the world moves.

2025: one person in five

Today, one out of every five people on Earth lives in Africa — 1.55 billion people. Nigeria alone has more people than Russia. The darker a country, the fewer people; the brighter, the more.

Countries shaded by total population, 2025. Non-African countries are muted for contrast but drawn on the same scale.

2100: nearly two in five

By 2100, Africa grows to 3.8 billion people — more than a third of everyone alive. Europe shrinks. Asia peaks around mid-century and turns down. Africa is the only region still climbing at the century's end, and the chart below shows it.

Regional totals, UN WPP 2024 medium variant.

The multiplication map

Now the colors show something different: how many times bigger each country gets between 2025 and 2100. The brightest countries nearly quadruple — DR Congo ×3.8, Angola ×3.8, Tanzania ×3.7. Blue countries actually shrink: most of Europe and East Asia will have fewer people in 2100 than today.

Chapter 2 / 4

Where the megacities rise

Now the people gather into cities. Every circle on the map is one city; the bigger the circle, the more people live there. The UN tracks each city from 1975 to 2050 — watch them grow as you scroll.

1975: a countryside continent

In 1975, only Cairo is truly big — 7 million people. Lagos has 2.5 million; Kinshasa barely 1 million. Most Africans still live in villages and small towns. For scale, four famous cities are drawn in gray on the same map.

African city
for comparison: New York, London, Paris, Tokyo

2025: the arrival

Fifty years later, Cairo holds 25 million, Lagos 13 million, Kinshasa 11 million — each already bigger than Paris or London. Look at the west coast: the cities between Abidjan and Lagos are growing into each other, forming one nearly continuous strip of city along the sea.

2050: past every Western city

By 2050, Cairo reaches 32 million and Luanda 20 million, while New York stays around 13 million. On UN numbers, 129 African cities will have passed a million people by mid-century — many of them places most Westerners have never heard of.

2100: cities like nothing before

The UN stops counting at 2050. But researchers who pushed the growth curves to 2100 found something remarkable: Lagos ~88 million, Kinshasa ~84 million, Dar es Salaam ~74 million — each one city holding more people than all of Germany. No city that size has ever existed.

The hollow rings show these 2100 estimates. They're drawn hollow on purpose: they are one research team's projection (Hoornweg & Pope, 2017), not an official count.

Chapter 3 / 4

Connecting a continent

Billions of people need ways to move between cities. This chapter looks at three maps: the roads and railways that exist, the ones being built right now, and the ones the future city map says will be needed.

What exists today

Africa's railway map is thin, and it mostly hugs the coasts. That's not an accident: most lines were built in colonial times to carry minerals from mines to ports — not to carry people between cities. Huge interior regions, including most of DR Congo, have no railway at all.

existing railway
major highway

What is being built — and who pays

The big new railways of the last decade — Mombasa to Nairobi, Addis Ababa to the port of Djibouti, Lagos to Ibadan — were financed and built by China, which sees Africa's growth coming and wants to be its builder and trade partner. In 2023 the US and Europe answered with the Lobito Corridor, a railway into the copper-mining heart of the continent. Who builds a country's infrastructure shapes who it trades with for decades — this map is geopolitics in concrete.

Where the traffic will want to go

Here's a simple prediction. Take the 30 biggest cities of 2050, and score every pair the way traffic engineers do: big city × big city ÷ distance² — the closer and bigger two cities are, the more travel and trade will flow between them. The strongest links, drawn in orange, sketch the corridors the future will demand: the west-coast arc, the Nile, a ladder of links through East Africa, Kinshasa–Luanda.

Chapter 4 / 4

Kinshasa, up close

Projections are abstract. So let's land in one city and watch this century actually happen, decade by decade, using satellite maps of what's been built.

Two capitals, one river

This is the Congo River widening into the Malebo Pool. On the south bank: Kinshasa, capital of DR Congo. On the north bank: Brazzaville, capital of the Republic of the Congo. They are the two closest capital cities in the world — about 4 km apart — and there is still no bridge between them.

Congo River / Malebo Pool

1975: the city they planned

In 1975 Kinshasa holds about a million people. The sand-colored area is everything that was built — you can still see the planned colonial city it grew from: a dense core on the riverbank and a street grid running south.

Built area mapped from satellite archives by the EU's Global Human Settlement Layer, in ~92-meter squares.

1990–2000: growth through the hardest years

These decades bring war and economic collapse — and the city grows enormously anyway: 3 million by 1990, 5 million by 2000. Almost none of it is planned. New neighborhoods build themselves, packing in tighter and climbing the hills south of the old core. The new rings of color show each decade's growth.

2010–2020: the megacity

10 million people by 2020. New construction surges east along the highway toward the airport, and the city's edge climbs hills never meant for streets. Yet look at the thin gray lines: most of those 10 million still move on a road network laid out when the city was a tenth this size.

2030 and beyond

The satellite mapping projects the built area out to 2030, and the UN expects 11.6 million people by then. The century-end research projection reaches ~84 million — a single city with more people than Germany, on the ground you just watched fill in.

The secret: packing in, not spreading out

Here's the surprise. The built area only grew about 1.5× since 1975 — but the ground that was already built in 1975 held 2.3 million people then, and holds 14 million today. Kinshasa and Brazzaville grew mostly by packing more than six times as many people onto the same land. The hottest colors below mark neighborhoods with over 60,000 people per square kilometre — more than five times as dense as New York City. The century of Africa gets decided in places exactly like this.
