The Century of Africa

Between now and 2100, most of the world's population growth happens on one continent. This is a tour of where the people, the cities, and the concrete will go — ending on the ground in Kinshasa.

The population century

UN World Population Prospects 2024, medium variant. Every country, 1950 to 2100. Watch the weight of the world move.

2025: one person in five

Africa holds 1.55 billion people today — about 19% of everyone alive. Nigeria, Ethiopia, Egypt and DR Congo are already among the world's most populous countries.

2100: nearly two in five

By century's end the UN's central estimate puts Africa at 3.8 billion — 37% of humanity. Europe shrinks. Asia peaks and turns down. Africa keeps climbing through the whole century.

Chapter 2 / 4

Where the megacities rise

Every circle is a city, sized by population. UN World Urbanization Prospects 2025 tracks each agglomeration from 1975 to 2050.

1975: a rural continent

Only Cairo clears 6 million. Lagos is 2.5M; Kinshasa barely 1M. Africa is still an overwhelmingly rural continent.

African agglomeration
benchmark: New York, London, Paris, Tokyo

2025: the arrival

Cairo 25M, Lagos 13M, Kinshasa 11M — already bigger than Paris or London. The Gulf of Guinea coast from Abidjan to Lagos is fusing into a near-continuous urban arc of tens of millions.

2050: past every Western city

Cairo reaches 32M, Luanda 20M, Lagos 16M, Dar es Salaam 13M — while New York sits near 13M and shrinking Tokyo near 31M. 129 African cities clear a million people by mid-century on UN numbers.

2100: cities with no precedent

Push the growth curves to century's end (Hoornweg & Pope, 2017) and Lagos ~88M, Kinshasa ~84M and Dar es Salaam ~74M become the largest cities that have ever existed — each bigger than all of today's Germany.

2100 circles are academic projections, not UN estimates — drawn hollow to say so.

Chapter 3 / 4

Connecting a continent

People at this scale need steel and asphalt. What exists, what is being financed, and what the 2050 city map says should exist.

What exists today

Africa's rail map is thin, coastal, and colonial — lines built to move minerals to ports, not people between cities. Vast interiors, including most of the DR Congo, have no railway at all.

What is being built — and who pays

China financed and built the marquee lines of the last decade: Mombasa–Nairobi, Addis–Djibouti, Lagos–Ibadan — on top of 1975's TAZARA. The West answered in 2023 with the Lobito Corridor into the copper belt. Infrastructure here is geopolitics in concrete.

Chapter 4 / 4

Kinshasa, ground truth

Across the Pool from Brazzaville — the closest pair of capitals on Earth — sits the city that shows what this century actually looks like from the street.

Two capitals, one pool

Kinshasa (south bank) and Brazzaville (north) face each other across the Malebo Pool of the Congo River — 4 km apart, the closest neighboring-country capitals in the world, and still not connected by a bridge.

Congo River / Malebo Pool

1975: the colonial footprint

Kinshasa holds a million people, still recognizably the planned city of Léopoldville: a dense riverside core and a grid running south. Satellite-mapped built fabric covers a fraction of today's extent.

Built-up footprint from the JRC Global Human Settlement Layer (GHS-BUILT-S), ~92 m cells, ≥20% built.

1990–2000: growth without a boom

Through war and collapse the city quintuples anyway — 3 million by 1990, 5.2 million by 2000. Almost all of it is informal: unplanned neighborhoods packing tighter and pushing up the hills south of the core.

2010–2020: the megacity

10 million by 2020. New fabric surges east along the N1 toward the airport and N'sele, and the sprawl frontier climbs terrain never meant for streets. Most trips still happen on a road network laid out for a city one-tenth the size.

2030 and the century beyond

GHSL projects the footprint to 2030; the UN sees 11.6M people by 2030 and Hoornweg & Pope's curve reaches ~84M by 2100 — a city the size of a mid-tier country, on the ground you just watched fill in. Density, not sprawl, has carried most of the load so far. The century of Africa gets decided in places exactly like this.
